<p>Quickmart is one of Kenya’s largest supermarket businesses. It has scale, growing profits and ambitious expansion plans.</p>

<p>But a good company is not automatically a good investment at any price.</p>

<p><strong>At KSh7.50 per share, I am not buying.</strong></p>

<section class="at-a-glance editorial-summary" aria-labelledby="article-at-a-glance"><p class="eyebrow" id="article-at-a-glance">AT A GLANCE</p><ul>

<li><strong>The business: </strong>Quickmart has grown to 72 stores, with annual revenue exceeding KSh50 billion and rising profits. But supermarket margins remain thin.</li>

<li><strong>The valuation: </strong>KSh7.50 values Quickmart at KSh30 billion — approximately 20 times reported 2025 earnings, or 17.6 times adjusted earnings.</li>

<li><strong>The dividend: </strong>The targeted FY2026 dividend of about KSh0.50 per share produces an estimated net yield of approximately 6.3% for a qualifying resident investor.</li>

<li><strong>The alternative: </strong>The referenced 364-day Treasury bill provides approximately 7.7% net, with substantially less business risk.</li>

<li><strong>The growth requirement: </strong>To justify accepting the lower initial income yield and greater equity risk, I need sufficient confidence that Quickmart can deliver meaningful earnings, dividend and capital growth.</li>

<li><strong>The cash question: </strong>Quickmart intends to expand while distributing a very high proportion of profits. Expansion, dividends and working-capital requirements will all compete for cash.</li>

<li><strong>The IPO structure: </strong>My KSh7.50 would buy existing shares. The IPO proceeds go to the selling shareholder rather than providing new capital to Quickmart.</li>

</ul><p class="editorial-bottom-line"><strong>BOTTOM LINE</strong><br /><strong>BOTTOM LINE
</strong><strong>Quickmart may be a good business. At KSh7.50, I do not think the prospective return adequately compensates me for the risks I would be taking.</strong></p></section>

## 1. The dividend does not compensate me sufficiently

<p>Quickmart is targeting approximately KSh2 billion of dividends for FY2026. With four billion shares outstanding, that represents approximately KSh0.50 per share.</p>

<p>At KSh7.50, the prospective gross dividend yield is about 6.7%. After the 5% withholding tax stated in Quickmart’s IPO materials for qualifying resident dividends, the prospective net cash yield is approximately 6.3%, assuming the targeted dividend is declared.</p>

<p>That is not unattractive in isolation. But investments do not exist in isolation.</p>

<p>The referenced 364-day Kenya Treasury bill offers approximately 7.7% net on the same simple comparison. So I would be accepting the risks of owning an equity investment while initially receiving less income than from a government security.</p>

<p>That does not mean Quickmart shares cannot outperform a Treasury bill. They certainly could. But the difference has to come from somewhere else. It has to come from growth.</p>

## 2. At KSh7.50, I am already paying for substantial future growth

<p>At KSh7.50 per share and four billion shares in issue, Quickmart is valued at KSh30 billion.</p>

<p>Against reported 2025 net profit of approximately KSh1.51 billion, that represents about 19.9 times earnings. Using adjusted profit after tax of approximately KSh1.7 billion reduces the multiple to about 17.6 times.</p>

<p>Neither multiple proves that Quickmart is overpriced. A rapidly growing company can justify a higher valuation.</p>

<p>But at close to 18–20 times recent earnings, I am not simply buying the business Quickmart is today. I am paying for a meaningful amount of what I expect it to become tomorrow.</p>

<p>Quickmart therefore needs to keep expanding, protect its margins and translate higher sales into materially higher profits. I am not sufficiently convinced that the potential upside at KSh7.50 compensates me for that execution risk.</p>

## 3. Supermarket growth does not automatically mean profit growth

<p>Quickmart reported approximately KSh50.43 billion of revenue in 2025, while reported net profit was approximately KSh1.51 billion. That implies a net margin of only about 3%.</p>

<p>This is not necessarily a criticism of Quickmart. Supermarkets are inherently high-volume, thin-margin businesses. But it matters enormously to an investor.</p>

<p>When margins are this narrow, relatively modest changes in costs, pricing, inventory losses, supplier terms or operating efficiency can have a disproportionate effect on profits.</p>

<p>Quickmart has demonstrated impressive growth. For me, the unresolved question is whether future expansion will produce enough additional profit and cash, rather than simply additional turnover, to justify today’s valuation.</p>

## 4. I am uncomfortable with the combination of expansion and very high dividends

<p>Quickmart intends to keep expanding. At the same time, its intended dividend payout is approximately 95% of profit after tax in FY2026, 90% in FY2027 and at least 80% thereafter, subject to profitability, cash requirements and board approval.</p>

<p>As a shareholder, a generous dividend sounds attractive. As an analyst, I have to ask where the cash for growth will come from.</p>

<p>Quickmart’s operating model helps answer that question. Customers generally pay immediately, while suppliers are paid later. As at June 2026, Quickmart reported structurally negative working capital of about KSh4 billion and only KSh6.8 million of conventional borrowings.</p>

<p>That can be an efficient supermarket financing model. But it also means that cash generation, inventory turnover and supplier terms remain important.</p>

<p>If Quickmart is going to expand aggressively and distribute most of its profits, I would want considerable confidence that those cash dynamics remain favourable. At KSh7.50, I do not think I am being given enough margin for that uncertainty.</p>

## 5. None of my KSh7.50 goes into Quickmart

<p>This IPO does not raise new equity capital for Quickmart.</p>

<p>The two billion shares being offered already exist. If fully sold at KSh7.50, gross proceeds would be KSh15 billion, with estimated net proceeds of about KSh14.4 billion going to Sokoni Retail Kenya, the selling shareholder.</p>

<p>There is nothing inherently wrong with an existing shareholder realising part of its investment. But it changes how I view the proposition.</p>

<p>My investment would buy an interest in the existing business. It would not inject fresh capital into Quickmart to finance the expansion on which part of my prospective return depends.</p>

<p>Future growth therefore has to be financed principally through Quickmart’s own cash generation and other available funding. That makes the previous question about dividends, working capital and expansion even more relevant.</p>

## 6. IFC’s investment is reassuring — but it doesn’t change my price

<p>IFC has conditionally agreed to invest approximately KSh1.94 billion, potentially acquiring around 6.45% of Quickmart at the same KSh7.50 price.</p>

<p>I regard that as positive. IFC has resources and due-diligence capabilities far beyond those of an individual retail investor.</p>

<p>But IFC and I do not necessarily have the same investment objectives, time horizon, portfolio or appetite for risk.</p>

<p>Its participation gives me greater confidence in the company and the transaction. It does not establish that KSh7.50 offers the return I require.</p>

## 7. Kenya’s supermarket history makes me cautious about cash

<p>Quickmart is not Nakumatt, Tuskys or Uchumi. It operates under different ownership and management, and it should be judged on its own merits.</p>

<p>Nevertheless, Kenya’s supermarket history provides a useful warning.</p>

<p>Large sales numbers and rapid expansion do not protect a retailer if working capital, suppliers, cash flow, governance or expansion economics deteriorate.</p>

<p>That is why, if I were a shareholder, I would watch Quickmart’s cash generation, supplier balances, inventory turnover and returns from new stores at least as closely as its headline revenue growth.</p>

## 8. Could another 800 million shares come onto the market?

<p>There is another reason for my caution: the possibility of additional shares being sold after listing.</p>

<p>If the IPO is fully subscribed, Sokoni Retail Kenya will retain two billion shares. Only 60% of that remaining holding — 1.2 billion shares — is subject to a 24-month lock-up, subject to the stated exceptions.</p>

<p>The remaining 800 million shares, equivalent to 20% of Quickmart’s issued share capital, would therefore be outside that lock-up. At the IPO price of KSh7.50, they have an indicative value of KSh6 billion.</p>

<p>No additional sale has been announced. The absence of a lock-up does not mean the shares will be sold.</p>

<p>Nevertheless, a sizeable future sale could put pressure on the market price or constrain potential appreciation if investor demand does not absorb the supply. It could also increase trading liquidity. Because these are existing shares, such a sale would not dilute shareholders.</p>

<p>At KSh7.50, that potential selling overhang is another risk I must weigh against the prospective return.</p>

## So why am I not buying?

<p>It is not because I think Quickmart is a bad company. Quite the opposite.</p>

<p>It is already a substantial Kenyan consumer business. It has more than KSh50 billion in annual sales, growing profits, little conventional borrowing, expansion potential and the endorsement implicit in IFC’s proposed investment.</p>

<p>My issue is price relative to return and risk.</p>

<ul>

<li>the prospective net dividend yield is below the referenced 364-day Treasury bill;</li>

<li>the valuation already requires meaningful future earnings growth;</li>

<li>supermarket margins remain thin;</li>

<li>expansion and very high dividend payouts both require cash;</li>

<li>supplier credit is an important component of the working-capital model; and</li>

<li>the IPO itself provides no fresh capital to Quickmart.</li>

<li>up to 800 million retained shares could potentially be sold without waiting for the 24-month lock-up to expire.</li>

</ul>

<p>For me, too much of the investment case therefore depends on future execution.</p>

<p>Quickmart could deliver that growth. If it does, shareholders could enjoy increasing dividends and capital appreciation.</p>

<p><strong>But at KSh7.50, I am being asked to pay today for too much of tomorrow’s success.</strong></p>

### My decision

<p><strong>I am not applying for Quickmart shares at KSh7.50.</strong></p>

<p>That is a decision about the price and the return I require — not a prediction that Quickmart will perform badly or that its share price will fall after listing.</p>

<p>A good company and a good investment are not necessarily the same thing. And a good investment at one price may not be a good investment at another.</p>

## Business Insights Addendum

<p><strong>What Quickmart’s IPO tells business owners and suppliers</strong></p>

<p>The investment decision ends above. But Quickmart’s IPO also provides useful lessons for people who may never buy a single Quickmart share.</p>

### Suppliers: watch the balance of power

<p>Quickmart’s negative working-capital model illustrates why supplier credit is valuable to a large retailer.</p>

<p>The retailer can sell goods and collect cash before paying the supplier. That reduces the amount of its own capital tied up in working capital.</p>

<p>For suppliers, however, the same arrangement means that their balance sheets are helping finance the retailer’s operations.</p>

<p>As Quickmart grows, suppliers may benefit from larger volumes. But they should watch payment periods, concentration of sales with one customer, margins and the amount of working capital required to support that growth.</p>

<p>More sales are not necessarily better business if they require disproportionately more financing.</p>

### Business owners: profit and cash are different things

<p>Quickmart also illustrates a broader lesson.</p>

<p>A company can be profitable and growing while still needing careful management of cash.</p>

<p>Expansion, dividends, inventory and supplier payments all ultimately compete for liquidity.</p>

<p>For any growing business, therefore, the question should not simply be: Are profits increasing?</p>

<p>It should also be: Where is the cash coming from, where is it going, and can the business continue financing its growth without creating financial strain?</p>

<p>That question applies far beyond supermarkets.</p>
