## AT A GLANCE

Tesco and Sainsbury's both increased sales, free cash flow and market share in 2025/26, yet their operating-profit trends differed, while both reported slightly lower returns on capital employed.

In Kenya, Kakuzi and Sasini both returned to profitability in 2025. Kakuzi generated KSh 853 million in operating cash flow, while Sasini absorbed KSh 708 million.

The differences matter to any business owner reviewing an apparently successful year. Higher sales and profits do not necessarily mean that capital is earning adequate returns, cash is being generated sustainably or the business has become stronger.

This article introduces a five-dimension **Business Improvement Scorecard**, applies it to the four companies and shows how the findings can shape three decisions for the next annual plan: **what to fix, what to protect and where to invest.**

---

## ANALYSIS

### What should an annual performance review measure?

Sales growth, profitability and cash flow are essential indicators. Their movements, however, need to be considered together.

A company may increase profit while investing more capital than the additional earnings justify. Cash flow may improve through better inventory management, or simply because maintenance and other necessary expenditure have been deferred.

The Business Improvement Scorecard examines five dimensions.

**Table 1 — The Business Improvement Scorecard**

| Dimension | What to assess | Evidence to examine |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Earnings quality** | Are recurring operating earnings and margins improving? | Customer and product contribution, margins, exceptional and non-cash gains |
| **2. Capital productivity** | Are assets and investments earning adequate returns? | Return on capital, asset utilisation, incremental investment returns and cost of capital |
| **3. Cash-generation quality** | Are earnings converting into cash sustainably? | Working capital, receivables, inventory, supplier terms and investment commitments |
| **4. Competitive strength** | Is the business winning and retaining profitable customers? | Market share, retention, pricing, customer profitability and service reliability |
| **5. Future capability and resilience** | Can the business sustain and extend its performance? | Maintenance, people, systems, capacity and operating risks |

A rating should reflect the direction of performance, the level reached and the factors responsible for the change. A modest decline in return on capital has different implications when earnings and asset utilisation are improving than when margins are falling and depreciation is increasing faster than operating earnings.

### Tesco and Sainsbury's: Similar sales growth, different profit outcomes

Both UK supermarket groups increased sales and gained market share during FY2025/26. Both also faced cost inflation, price competition and continuing pressure to invest in customer value.

**Table 2 — UK supermarket performance**

| Performance measure | Tesco | Sainsbury's |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales growth, excluding fuel | +4.6% | +4.3% |
| Sales growth, including fuel | +3.6% | +2.8% |
| Adjusted/underlying operating-profit growth | +0.8% | −1.1% |
| Operating margin, previous → current | 4.47% → 4.35% | 3.17% → 3.06% |
| Return on capital employed | 14.6% → 14.4% | 9.0% → 8.9% |
| Free cash flow | £1.75bn → £1.96bn | £531m → £574m |

*Sources: Published FY2025/26 results. Tesco's measures principally relate to the group, while Sainsbury's operating-profit and free-cash-flow measures relate to retail operations. Operating margins use revenue including fuel. Company definitions differ, so comparisons are principally within each company.*

#### Tesco

Tesco increased adjusted operating profit, gained market share and generated more free cash flow. Its *Save to Invest* programme supported cost efficiency and helped fund continued investment in customer value.

Operating profit rose only 0.8%, however, against sales growth of 3.6% including fuel. Its calculated operating margin declined from 4.47% to 4.35%, while return on capital employed eased from 14.6% to 14.4%.

Tesco's commercial execution was strong, but I would rate its earnings quality as mixed. Higher profit on substantially greater sales does not constitute improved margin performance, although the deterioration was modest.

The returns from continuing investment in distribution, technology and customer value will determine whether Tesco can translate its commercial progress into stronger capital productivity.

#### Sainsbury's

Sainsbury's grocery sales increased by 5.2%, supported by volume growth and market-share gains. The 4.3% growth shown in Table 2 covers total retail sales excluding fuel, including Argos and other non-grocery activities.

Underlying retail operating profit declined by 1.1%, despite substantial cost savings.

Retail underlying EBITDA increased by £19 million, but depreciation and amortisation rose by £30 million, leaving operating profit £11 million lower. EBITDA margin fell from 6.72% to 6.59%, while return on capital employed declined from 9.0% to 8.9%.

To me, this looks as much like a capital-returns problem as a pricing problem. Sainsbury's has increased grocery volumes and strengthened its competitive position, but the additional business has yet to produce higher operating earnings on the capital committed.

Management's investment in customer value may prove worthwhile over a longer period. The current results leave that case unproven.

#### Cash generation needs a separate assessment

Both companies increased free cash flow, but their cash-flow statements reveal a substantial contribution from working capital.

Tesco's free cash flow increased by £207 million to £1,957 million. Its working-capital cash movement changed from a £45 million outflow in 2024/25 to a £385 million inflow in 2025/26, a favourable swing of £430 million. Cash generated from retail operations before working-capital changes increased by £92 million, but higher capital expenditure, tax payments and other outflows offset part of the working-capital benefit.

Tesco attributes the inflow partly to higher trade payables associated with sales growth, working-capital management and a payable relating to the new Extended Producer Responsibility levy. These factors need to be considered when assessing how much of the cash-flow improvement is repeatable.

Sainsbury's retail free cash flow increased by £43 million. Cash generated before working-capital movements rose by £33 million, from £2,259 million to £2,292 million. Favourable working-capital movements increased from £98 million to £128 million, contributing an additional £30 million.

That £30 million is equivalent to approximately 70% of the increase in free cash flow. Other cash-flow changes also affected the final result, so the percentage is a measure of relative size rather than a complete attribution of the increase.

I would rate **cash-generation quality as mixed for both companies**. Each generated more free cash, but working-capital movements made a material contribution. Their sustainability matters as much as the reported increase.

#### My assessment

Tesco delivered the stronger overall operating performance in 2025/26. Its adjusted profit grew modestly, while Sainsbury's underlying retail operating profit declined.

Both strengthened their competitive positions, although neither improved capital returns. Working capital flattered both cash-flow figures, and at Tesco the £430 million favourable swing was larger than the entire £207 million increase in free cash flow.

For manufacturers, retailers and distributors, the issue is familiar. Additional customers and sales volumes are valuable when the contribution earned justifies the pricing concessions, assets and working capital required to support them.

### Kakuzi and Sasini: Profit recovery and capital returns

Both Kenyan agricultural companies reported a substantial recovery in profitability during 2025. Their results look different when biological-asset valuations, cash conversion and capital returns are examined.

**Table 3 — Kenyan performance, 2025**

| Measure | Kakuzi | Sasini |
|---|---:|---:|
| Reported pre-tax profit, KSh million | +568.4 | +344.0 |
| Indicative pre-tax result excluding identified biological-asset valuation movements, KSh million | +504.5 | −214.7 |
| Net operating cash flow, KSh million | +853.2 | −707.9 |
| Indicative ROCE — reported operating-profit basis | +7.4% | +1.6% |
| Indicative ROCE — after identified valuation adjustments | +6.5% | −0.6% |

*Sources: Audited 2025 financial statements. The earnings adjustments and ROCE calculations are independent illustrative measures, not company-reported underlying results. Sasini's profit and cash-flow figures relate to continuing operations. Calculations and limitations appear in Financial Annexure A.*

#### Kakuzi

Kakuzi's profit recovery remains substantial after adjusting for two identified biological-asset valuation movements. Its adjusted pre-tax result improved from a KSh 143.5 million loss in 2024 to a KSh 504.5 million profit in 2025.

Operating cash flow rose from approximately KSh 475 million to KSh 853 million, without a significant contribution from asset disposals or a release of working capital.

Its calculated operating return on capital also improved from negative 3.5% to positive 7.4%. Taken together, these figures provide convincing evidence of recovery.

There are qualifications to both earnings and cash generation. Operating cash included KSh 69.7 million of interest received, while cash tax payments were substantially below the year's income-tax expense. Improved agricultural production and stronger macadamia prices also contributed to profitability.

The capital-return figure deserves scrutiny. Kakuzi's 7.4% return represents considerable progress, but I would hesitate to call it satisfactory without examining the cost of capital and longer-term asset productivity.

Kakuzi has clearly improved. Whether it now earns an adequate return on the capital employed remains open, as discussed in the capital-returns comparison below.

#### Sasini

Sasini reported pre-tax profit of KSh 344 million, including KSh 558.8 million in biological-asset fair-value gains. Excluding the separately reported gain produces an indicative pre-tax loss of KSh 214.7 million.

The improvement from the comparable adjusted loss of approximately KSh 681 million in 2024 is substantial. Its remaining reported income and expenses moved in the right direction.

The cash-flow position is less encouraging. Inventories and trade and other receivables absorbed more than KSh 1 billion combined, while net operating cash flow from continuing operations fell from positive KSh 308.7 million in 2024 to negative KSh 707.9 million in 2025.

Sasini's indicative ROCE improved from negative 3.3% to positive 1.6%. Excluding the identified biological-asset gain from operating profit produces a negative indicative return of approximately 0.6%.

The company's capital base was materially affected by property revaluations and includes assets associated with discontinued operations. These limitations make the calculated returns less precise as measures of operating productivity.

I would regard Sasini's results as progress in a recovery rather than a completed turnaround. Its capital-return position is considerably weaker than Kakuzi's, even though both receive a mixed scorecard assessment. The improvement in adjusted earnings is meaningful, but the substantial cash absorption and weak capital returns remain concerns.

#### Assessing the adequacy of capital returns

The 7.4% and 1.6% calculated returns for Kakuzi and Sasini are both below the approximately 9.37% weighted-average accepted rate on Kenya's 364-day Treasury bill issue dated 8 December 2025.

This is a rough late-2025 market reference. Kakuzi's financial year ended in December, while Sasini's ended in September, three months before the Treasury bill issue. Neither company's operating return is directly comparable with a government security. Agricultural business risks, different asset bases and company-specific capital costs require separate consideration.

Kakuzi's capital productivity recovered substantially, while Sasini's remained weak. Neither return can be described as adequate solely because it improved from a loss.

## THE SCORECARD APPLIED

### What do the four companies' results reveal?

**Table 4 — Applied Business Improvement Scorecard**

| Dimension | Tesco | Sainsbury's | Kakuzi | Sasini |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Earnings quality** | Mixed | Deteriorated | Improved | Mixed |
| **Capital productivity** | Mixed | Deteriorated | Mixed | Mixed |
| **Cash-generation quality** | Mixed | Mixed | Improved | Deteriorated |
| **Competitive strength** | Improved | Improved | Not assessed | Not assessed |
| **Future capability and resilience** | Not assessed | Not assessed | Not assessed | Not assessed |

*Independent assessments for the financial periods examined. "Mixed" indicates conflicting favourable and adverse evidence; "Not assessed" indicates insufficient public information for a reliable judgement. The ratings are not company-issued assessments.*

**Tesco:** Sales, adjusted operating profit and free cash flow increased, but operating margin and ROCE declined slightly. A substantial favourable working-capital movement contributed to the free-cash-flow increase, supporting a mixed assessment of cash-generation quality despite the higher reported figure.

**Sainsbury's:** Market share and free cash flow improved, but underlying retail operating profit, EBITDA margin and ROCE declined. Its cash-flow improvement also benefited materially from a larger working-capital inflow. These factors support deterioration in earnings quality and capital productivity, with a mixed assessment for cash-generation quality.

**Kakuzi:** Earnings and cash generation improved significantly. The recovery in calculated capital returns was substantial, although their adequacy has not been demonstrated. Its capital-productivity rating remains mixed for that reason.

**Sasini:** Adjusted earnings improved but remained negative. Capital returns recovered from a loss-making position yet remained weak, considerably below Kakuzi's calculated return, while operating cash flow deteriorated sharply. The mixed earnings and capital assessments recognise progress alongside the remaining weaknesses.

### Why there is no overall numerical rating

Adding the five ratings into a composite score would require assigning weights to measures that may be closely related.

A reduction in inventory, for example, may improve cash flow while causing stock shortages and lost customers. Deferred maintenance may temporarily improve profits but increase equipment failures later.

The review needs to examine the decisions producing these effects and their overall economic consequences.

Published accounts also provide limited evidence of operational resilience. The four-company assessment cannot reliably determine whether maintenance, employee capability, technology systems or production reliability improved. Those matters require internal operating information.

For each material finding, I would record what changed, the underlying cause, whether the movement is sustainable and the decision that follows.

## FROM FINDINGS TO ACTION

The scorecard becomes useful when its findings influence the decisions made by management.

**Table 5 — Findings and management responses**

| Performance finding | Management response | Evidence to monitor |
|---|---|---|
| Sales rising, contribution margins falling | Review pricing, discounts and customer profitability | Contribution by customer and product |
| Profits rising, capital returns declining | Examine recent investments and underutilised assets | Incremental returns and utilisation |
| Profits improving, cash conversion weakening | Investigate inventories, credit terms and collections | Receivable ageing, stock quality and cash conversion |
| Market share increasing, profitability declining | Test the economics of customer acquisition and retention | Customer contribution and repeat purchasing |
| Current profits improving, operational reliability weakening | Review maintenance, employee capability and operating systems | Downtime, service failures and maintenance backlog |

An adverse indicator does not always warrant immediate corrective action. Higher inventories may support profitable seasonal demand, while lower current returns may follow investment in capacity that has yet to reach efficient utilisation.

### A distributor's credit policy

Consider a distributor that extends credit to larger customers. Sales and operating profit increase, but receivables grow faster than revenue and additional borrowing becomes necessary.

The first response might be to tighten credit or accelerate collections. I would start by examining the economics of those customers: their contribution after delivery costs, financing costs, collection risks and other resources required to service them.

If the incremental return justifies the additional capital, the credit policy may be commercially sound. Otherwise, management can renegotiate terms, revise prices, reduce exposure to low-return customers or redirect resources towards more profitable accounts.

The choice depends on the incremental economics of the business being won.

## THREE DECISIONS BEFORE THE NEXT ANNUAL PLAN

The annual performance review should conclude with a limited number of priorities that management is prepared to implement.

### What must we fix?

Identify the weakness most likely to damage sustainable returns. It may involve customer profitability, excessive working capital, poor asset utilisation or unreliable operations. Examine the cause and agree on corrective action.

### What must we protect?

Identify the capabilities and competitive advantages supporting performance. These may include customer relationships, product quality, distribution reliability, experienced employees and effective information systems. Protect them when considering cost reductions or operational changes.

### Where should we invest?

Choose opportunities with credible prospects for improving future returns. Assess the expected benefits, capital requirements, implementation risks and milestones before committing resources.

Each priority should have an accountable executive, a defined action, a measurable outcome, agreed resources and a review date. Targets should reflect the business's circumstances and economics rather than arbitrary percentages.

An initial review after approximately 90 days can assess progress against implementation milestones and the assumptions behind the decisions. Investments requiring longer periods should be monitored through an appropriate sequence of reviews.

## CONCLUSION

Tesco and Sainsbury's show how similar commercial progress can produce different operating-profit and capital-return outcomes. Kakuzi and Sasini demonstrate how biological-asset valuations, working-capital movements and the capital employed can change the interpretation of a reported profit recovery.

The five-dimension scorecard provides a basis for examining these measures together, while identifying where the accounts leave important questions unanswered.

An annual review should give management a clear understanding of the business's strengths, weaknesses and capacity to earn sustainable returns. Its findings should determine where attention, resources and capital are directed next.

Before approving the next annual plan, every owner and CEO should be able to answer:

**What are the three most important decisions we will make differently because of what we learned from this year's performance?**

If the review cannot answer that question, it remains unfinished.

## METHODOLOGY & DISCLOSURE

The analysis uses published company results and audited financial statements. Tesco and Sainsbury's apply different reporting definitions for certain performance measures. Kakuzi and Sasini differ in financial year-ends, agricultural activities, asset bases and accounting treatments.

The Kenyan earnings adjustments and ROCE calculations are independent illustrative analyses, not company-reported measures of underlying performance. Excluding selected biological-asset fair-value movements does not eliminate every valuation effect or establish sustainable earnings. These movements may reflect genuine economic changes.

The December 2025 Kenyan Treasury bill rate provides historical market context only. It is not a company-specific cost-of-capital calculation and is not contemporaneous with Sasini's September year-end.

The assessments concern the financial periods examined and are subject to the limitations of publicly available information.

This article is independent business commentary, not a recommendation to buy or sell securities.

## What Would an Independent Review Reveal About Your Business?

Your annual performance review should identify what has improved, where weaknesses remain and which management decisions require attention. That requires examining the reasons behind financial results and their implications for future performance.

Drawing on more than 35 years of senior executive experience, I undertake selected remote advisory assignments through *Murtaza Insights*, covering **Business Performance Reviews, Business Analysis & Strategy, and Information Systems & Process Improvement**.

The focus is on independent assessment, practical judgement and clear management priorities.
