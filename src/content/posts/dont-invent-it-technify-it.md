Walk through a traditional market in almost any tourist destination and something interesting happens.

You ask the price.

The trader looks at you.

He hears your accent. Notices what you are wearing. Perhaps sees the hotel wristband on your arm. He decides how badly you appear to want the item.

Then he gives you a price.

A local resident may be quoted something quite different.

There is nothing remotely new about this. Traders have been estimating what individual customers might be willing to pay for centuries.

Now replace the trader’s judgement with software.

Give it information about browsing behaviour, previous purchases, location or buying patterns.

Suddenly we have what sounds like a thoroughly modern concept:

personalised pricing.

In 2026, the US Federal Trade Commission described personalised pricing as the use of personal data to set a price according to what a business believes an individual consumer is willing to spend. The FTC has also been examining the transparency and consumer-protection implications of such practices.

Cutting-edge technology?

Certainly.

Entirely new commercial thinking?

Not really.

It is the old market trader — technified.

## Perhaps technology is not always inventing new business

We tend to think of innovation as creating something that has never existed before.

But there may be another route.

Look backwards.

Find a business practice that worked well for decades, perhaps centuries, and ask:

What stopped this from becoming much bigger?

Often the answer is surprisingly mundane.

- It required too much human time.
- It depended on one person’s memory.
- It only worked locally.
- Finding the right buyer or seller was difficult.
- Negotiation took too long.
- Records were manual.
- Coordination became impossible once too many people were involved.

Technology may not need to reinvent the underlying idea.

It only needs to remove the constraint.

That distinction matters.

## Take haggling

Negotiation is one of the oldest forms of commerce.

A trader says:

KES 10,000.

You say:

KES 7,000.

He counters.

You counter.

Eventually, perhaps, you shake hands at KES 8,500.

Then e-commerce arrived and, strangely, removed most of that interaction.

The internet gave us two choices:

BUY

or

DON’T BUY

But automated negotiation is now bringing some of the old behaviour back.

Nibble, for example, uses AI-supported negotiation systems that can negotiate prices and commercial terms while operating within rules set by the business. Its systems are used in areas including e-commerce and procurement.

So AI did not invent haggling.

It made it possible for a business to haggle with large numbers of customers or suppliers simultaneously.

The bazaar has returned.

Only the trader is now software.

## The neighbourhood shopkeeper was doing data analytics too

Think about the traditional neighbourhood shopkeeper.

A good one knew his customers remarkably well.

He knew:

- what they normally bought;
- who always paid promptly;
- who could be trusted with a little credit;
- which customer preferred which brand;
- who normally appeared every Friday;
- and who had not been seen for a few weeks.

He did not call it customer relationship management.

He did not have a dashboard.

He simply remembered.

His problem was scale.

One person may know a few hundred customers exceptionally well.

Software can potentially analyse millions of interactions.

Modern CRM systems, recommendation engines and AI therefore do something surprisingly familiar:

they try to recreate the memory and judgement of a very good shopkeeper.

The concept is ancient.

The processing power is new.

## Consider the buying club

Neighbours and businesses have pooled their purchasing power for generations.

One family buying ten litres of cooking oil has limited bargaining power.

One hundred families buying a thousand litres collectively have rather more.

The principle is obvious.

The problem has always been administration.

- Someone must gather the orders.
- Collect the money.
- Confirm quantities.
- Find suppliers.
- Negotiate.
- Distribute the goods.
- Keep records.

Technology can automate much of that coordination.

The underlying business idea has not changed at all.

What was once a manually organised buying club could become a continuously operating digital marketplace in which aggregated demand gives buyers greater negotiating power.

Again:

old idea, new scale.

## Barter has the opposite problem

Barter is one of humanity’s oldest forms of exchange.

Its weakness is equally old.

I have something you want.

But you may not have something I want.

Traditionally, that kills the trade.

Software can search for combinations that would be almost impossible for individuals to discover manually.

Perhaps:

- I want what Amina has.
- Amina wants what Peter has.
- Peter wants what I have.

The idea of exchanging goods is ancient.

The ability to identify complex chains across thousands of participants is not.

That is another example of an old commercial system becoming interesting again because technology can remove the old constraint.

## The pawnbroker had another valuable skill

A traditional pawnbroker could inspect a watch, piece of jewellery, television or tool and quickly estimate:

- what it was;
- its likely condition;
- what someone else might pay for it;
- and therefore how much could safely be lent against it.

That judgement came from experience.

Now imagine taking a photograph of the item.

Software identifies the product.

An AI vision system assesses visible characteristics.

The application checks recent market information and the business’s own pricing rules.

The customer could then receive an indicative valuation or offer.

What happened?

The pawnbroker’s business model did not disappear.

Part of the pawnbroker’s expertise became software.

## And this is where AI changes the equation

Until quite recently, somebody with such an idea faced another problem.

Who is going to build it?

You might need programmers.

Software engineers.

Specialist AI developers.

Servers.

Infrastructure.

Considerable upfront money.

That barrier is falling.

A business building an AI application does not normally have to create its own large language model from scratch.

It can connect its software to an AI provider through an API — essentially allowing the application to send information to an AI model, receive a result and incorporate that result into the service presented to the customer.

The developer pays for the AI that is used.

OpenAI, for example, provides models through its developer platform with usage-based pricing rather than requiring each company to build its own model infrastructure. Image inputs can similarly be analysed through vision-capable models and charged according to usage.

Put more simply:

You can rent the intelligence.

That changes the economics considerably.

## And you may not have to write all the code either

There is a second development that may prove just as important.

AI can increasingly help build the application that will itself use AI.

Modern coding agents can generate, review, modify and debug software. OpenAI, for example, describes code generation and software-engineering assistance as core uses of its coding systems.

That does not mean professional programmers have become unnecessary.

Real applications still require testing, security, integration, reliability, privacy controls and sound technical judgement.

But it does mean something important for entrepreneurs.

You no longer necessarily need to begin with:

“I have an idea. Now I need to find somebody who can code it.”

You may be able to begin with:

“I understand the problem. Let me build a simple version and see whether the idea works.”

You can test a working prototype in a weekend rather than spending months searching for a technical co-founder.

For a younger entrepreneur with limited capital, that is significant.

For an established business owner, it may be even more useful.

You do not necessarily need to launch a major “AI transformation programme”.

You might simply test whether technology can improve one old process.

## That may be the real opportunity for established businesses

For CEOs and owners of established companies, the useful question is probably not:

“How do we become an AI company?”

Most businesses do not need to become AI companies.

A more practical question is:

“Which part of our business still works roughly the way it did 20 years ago because changing it was previously too difficult or expensive?”

Perhaps it is:

- quotations;
- supplier negotiation;
- stock clearance;
- customer follow-up;
- credit assessment;
- appointment scheduling;
- purchasing;
- document review;
- matching buyers with sellers;
- answering repetitive customer questions;
- or analysing information that presently sits in several different systems.

These are not glamorous problems.

They are often exactly where the money is.

If technology saves staff time, reduces stock, improves margin, accelerates cash collection or makes customers easier to serve, the project does not need to be revolutionary.

It simply needs to make commercial sense.

## Start with the business practice, not the AI

This may be where many businesses get the sequence wrong.

They begin with:

“We need to use AI.”

Then they search for something for AI to do.

Try reversing the process.

Look at the business first.

Identify something that works — but only because somebody spends considerable time, memory, judgement or effort making it work.

Then ask:

If technology removed that limitation, what would become possible?

That is a rather different way of approaching innovation.

And it leads to another question:

What did an excellent trader, shopkeeper, broker, buyer, accountant or salesman do 50 or 100 years ago that technology could now do at scale?

That may be a surprisingly fertile place to look for the next business opportunity.

## Not everything should be technified

There is an important qualification.

An old practice may also contain an old problem.

Return to our market trader.

When everyone knows that bargaining is taking place, negotiation is transparent.

If software quietly charges different customers different prices because it has analysed their personal behaviour, the issue becomes more complicated.

Customers may reasonably want to know how such decisions are being made.

That is precisely why regulators are examining personalised pricing and the use of personal data in setting prices.

Technology amplifies good practices.

It can amplify bad ones too.

So the question is not merely:

Can this be technified?

It should also be:

Should it be?

And:

Does it create genuine value for the business and its customer?

## Look backwards before looking forwards

Somewhere inside an old marketplace, shop, warehouse, purchasing department or office may be a practice that has survived for decades because it works.

It may still depend on:

- memory.
- judgement.
- matching.
- negotiation.
- coordination.
- repetition.

Those happen to be areas where software and AI can increasingly provide leverage.

The next useful business application therefore may not begin by asking:

“What completely new thing can AI invent?”

It may begin with a much simpler question:

“What already works — but has never been able to scale?”

Then do not necessarily reinvent it.

Technify it.
