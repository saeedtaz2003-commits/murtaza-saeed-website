**Quickmart may be a good business, but that does not automatically make it a good investment at every price.**

Read on if you want to understand:

- where the IPO money goes;
- profit and margin quality;
- supplier financing, negative working capital and what happens if payment terms tighten;
- whether a 90%+ dividend payout can coexist with expansion;
- valuation, P/E, implied market value and dividend yield;
- the Treasury-bill comparison;
- expansion and lease risks; and
- what to check in the final Information Memorandum.

## What exactly is being offered?

Current shareholders plan to sell two billion existing shares, representing 50% of Quickmart, with provision for an additional 7.5% if demand is strong. Investors would buy shares from current owners rather than subscribe for new shares issued by the company.

The proceeds therefore go to the selling shareholders. **Quickmart itself receives no fresh equity from this IPO** to finance stores, stock or expansion. A shareholder exit is not inherently a problem, but the investment case must stand on the company's existing finances and future cash generation.

## How strong is the underlying business?

FY2025 revenue was about KSh50.4 billion, with reported profit after tax of approximately KSh1.51 billion and adjusted profit of about KSh1.71 billion. H1 2026 revenue was about KSh27.3 billion, with profit after tax of approximately KSh873 million.

That represents meaningful scale and encouraging momentum. But the reported FY2025 net margin was only about **3%**: roughly three shillings of profit for every hundred shillings of sales. Small changes in purchasing costs, wastage, rent, payroll or finance costs can therefore have a substantial effect on profit.

Adjusted profit also deserves scrutiny. Investors should understand the reconciliation to reported earnings and whether the excluded costs are genuinely exceptional.

<h2 id="supplier-credit">The KSh4 billion question — who finances the shelves?</h2>

Supplier credit helps supermarkets finance inventory. Customers may pay for goods before the retailer pays its suppliers. This can create negative working capital, where operating current liabilities exceed operating current assets. It can be efficient, provided suppliers remain willing to support the cycle.

The roughly KSh4 billion working-capital question is therefore about the resilience of this financing arrangement, not simply whether a negative balance is good or bad. Look at inventory, trade payables, payment periods and operating cash flow together.

If suppliers shorten payment periods, Quickmart must pay cash earlier. More cash becomes tied up financing stock. If internal cash is insufficient, overdrafts or short-term borrowing may be needed. Those facilities create interest and finance costs, reducing profits and the cash generated by operations and leaving less for dividends and expansion. The precise cash-flow statement classification of interest depends on the accounting policy.

> **Shorter supplier terms → more cash required for working capital → increased borrowing → higher interest and finance costs → lower profits and weaker cash flow → less cash available for dividends and expansion.**

Stable supplier relationships and available liquidity facilities are consequently as important as sales growth.

<h2 id="dividends-and-growth">Attractive dividends — but can Quickmart afford both dividends and growth?</h2>

Projected dividends are about KSh2 billion for FY2026 and KSh2.5 billion for FY2027, with payout ratios of approximately 95.1% and 90%. With four billion shares, the FY2026 projection implies about **KSh0.50 per share**.

These are forecasts, not guaranteed distributions. Paying out most earnings leaves relatively little retained profit for expansion. New stores require fit-outs, deposits, inventory, staff and pre-opening expenditure. Investors need a clear explanation of how growth will be funded through operating cash generation, supplier credit, leases or borrowing, especially because this share sale does not inject new equity.

## What about Treasury bills?

The approximately 9.0% 364-day Treasury-bill rate used in this analysis provides a useful income benchmark. A projected KSh0.50 dividend gives a yield above that benchmark at KSh4–5, but below it at KSh6–7.

The comparison is not like for like. A Treasury bill has a defined maturity and government credit exposure; shares have uncertain dividends and no fixed repayment value. Shares also offer possible capital appreciation, alongside the risk of capital loss. Compare applicable tax treatment, investment horizon and net returns, and recheck Treasury-bill rates when the offer opens.

At KSh7, more of the expected return would need to come from earnings growth and capital appreciation. Post-listing prices will also reflect sentiment, liquidity and wider NSE conditions, while long-term value remains connected to business performance.

## What else could change the picture?

- **Lease commitments:** rents and lease liabilities remain obligations even when a store underperforms.
- **Expansion returns:** additional stores should generate worthwhile returns, rather than simply increase revenue.
- **Thin margins:** cost increases and stock losses can quickly erode profitability.
- **Supplier relationships:** credit availability and reliable deliveries underpin the operating model.

<h2 id="final-checks">What am I waiting for before reaching a final view?</h2>

The final Information Memorandum should settle the offer price and provide the latest earnings, operating cash flow, reported-versus-adjusted profit reconciliation, supplier-payment terms, lease liabilities, dividend assumptions and expansion funding. Those details matter more than an attractive headline yield alone.

## My assessment

My position is interested, but price-dependent. Around KSh4–5, the earnings multiple and projected income offer a stronger starting point. Around KSh6–7, I would need greater confidence that growth and dividends can be sustained without putting pressure on the balance sheet.

The decision should follow the final offer document and price, with particular attention to cash remaining after supplier payments, expansion and financing obligations.
