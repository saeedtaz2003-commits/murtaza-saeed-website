The normal reaction to a price reduction is straightforward:

Lower prices mean lower profits.

Often they do.

But not necessarily.

A lower price can improve the overall result if it brings in enough additional customers — provided the business also has an economical way of reaching and serving that higher volume.

Simbisa Kenya provides an interesting real-life example.

## Lower spend, but many more customers

During FY2026, Simbisa Kenya’s average customer spend fell by 3%.

That sounds negative until you look at what happened alongside it.

Customer volumes increased by 14%, helping revenue grow by 11%.

Simbisa described this as a value-led pricing strategy. It had responded to increasingly price-conscious customers by introducing more affordable value-meal options.

So customers spent slightly less on each transaction — but substantially more customers bought from Simbisa.

That is the first part of the equation.

The question for another business is not simply:

Should I reduce my prices?

It is:

If I reduce my price, how much additional volume would I need to make the reduction worthwhile?

But that immediately leads to another question:

How will I reach and serve all those additional customers?

## The second change: another route to the customer

This is where Simbisa’s delivery strategy becomes particularly relevant.

Delivery orders in Kenya increased by 59% during FY2026.

Its delivery channels included established third-party platforms such as Glovo, Uber Eats and Bolt Food, alongside its own delivery operation.

That matters.

Simbisa did not have to rely only on more customers physically visiting its restaurants. Nor did every additional delivery depend on Simbisa building the entire ordering and delivery infrastructure itself.

Established platforms already had technology, ordering systems, customers and delivery infrastructure.

They are not free — there are commissions and other costs.

But using somebody else’s established channel can be more cost-effective than trying to recreate the same reach and infrastructure yourself.

## The two changes work together

That is the more useful lesson from the Simbisa example.

Reducing price alone is not the strategy.

If the price reduction generates more demand, the business must also be able to accommodate that extra demand economically.

In Simbisa’s case, two things were happening at the same time:

Better value helped attract more customers.

Expanding delivery channels helped the business reach and serve more customers.

The FY2026 outcome was:

| Measure | Change |
| --- | --- |
| Average customer spend | -3% |
| Customer volumes | +14% |
| Revenue | +11% |
| Delivery orders | +59% |
| Operating profit | +16% |

Simbisa also attributes its profit performance to operating efficiencies and cost management, although its Kenya results do not disclose enough detail for us to analyse precisely how those savings were achieved.

So we should not pretend there was only one reason for the improvement.

But the interaction between price, volume and route to the customer is clear.

## What is the equivalent in your business?

You may not sell fast food.

You may not need Glovo, Uber Eats or Bolt Food.

But the same two questions may still be worth asking:

Could reducing your price bring in enough additional customers to improve the overall result?

And, if it did:

Is there already somebody with the customers, technology, distribution network or marketplace you could use rather than building the entire channel yourself?

The answer might be a distributor, marketplace, reseller, agent, online platform or another form of partnership.

Sometimes improving performance is not about making one dramatic change.

It is about finding two changes that work together.
